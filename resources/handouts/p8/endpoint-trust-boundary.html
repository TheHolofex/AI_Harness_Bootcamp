Know what lies behind the endpoint name

An endpoint looks like one destination. Operationally, it is a chain of components, identities, stores, people, policies, and organizations. A request may cross a gateway, provider ingress, inference worker, telemetry system, support console, and backup process. The endpoint label reveals little of that.

Plan for 60–75 minutes. You will leave with an endpoint data-flow and trust-boundary dossier for one proposed endpoint arrangement. It will record the flows, credentials, transport, egress, logs, retention, backups, provider or administrator access, locality and jurisdiction claims, controls, evidence, owners, and unresolved questions. The dossier does not approve an endpoint. It makes the approval question inspectable.

Begin after P8 · Govern before touch, which establishes the acceptable-use decision and run response. Go one layer behind the destination: what actually receives, moves, observes, stores, or can access the request? Analyze one arrangement, not an endpoint portfolio.

Your finish line A peer can trace a request and its credentials through every known boundary, distinguish a control from a claim about that control, find each supporting artifact, see who owns every open question, and tell whether you have cleared only a synthetic probe, held sensitive use, or stopped for an authority decision.

Trace four planes, then test every claim

For this dossier, treat a trust boundary as a crossing where administrative control, acting identity, governing policy, or available evidence changes. A network perimeter can be one crossing, but it is not the whole map. NIST SP 800-207 states that network or physical location and asset ownership do not by themselves create trust. A private address, local gateway, or organization-owned account therefore answers a placement question; it does not settle who can process, observe, administer, or retain the data.

This four-plane view is a course field method, not a named NIST model. The NIST Privacy Framework calls for inventorying data actions, elements, purposes, environments, owners, operators, and third-party interactions. NIST SP 800-53 adds least privilege, authenticator management, logging, backups, transmission protection, disposal, and external-service oversight.

Run the boundary pass

  1. Freeze the unit and data class. Name one use, one endpoint arrangement, and the most sensitive data element that could enter it. “Our AI service” is too broad. “Draft a maintenance after-action report from a work-order excerpt through the organization gateway” is traceable.
  2. Draw known components and every outbound edge. Start at the person or source system. Include identity, secret and key stores, telemetry, support, subprocessors, and recovery stores when they participate. Mark an absent component UNKNOWN.
  3. Annotate each crossing. Record the data or credential, purpose, component owners, acting principal, transport protection, and egress destination. Transit encryption does not prevent authorized plaintext processing at either end.
  4. Trace persistence and human access. Ask separately about content and metadata logs, safety records, error traces, caches, replicas, backups, administrators, support, and emergency access. An invisible log is not a nonexistent log.
  5. Split locality and control. Record compute, persistence, administrative, legal or organizational, and evidence locality. “Local” can be true for one and unknown for another. A customer-managed storage key does not establish protection for memory, telemetry, support, or backups.
  6. Convert prose into dossier rows. Write the exact claim, the mechanism that would make it true, the artifact that supports it, the artifact owner and date, and what remains unanswered. If one row needs several meanings of “private,” split it.
  7. Issue a bounded status. Use CLEAR FOR SYNTHETIC PROBE, HOLD SENSITIVE USE, or STOP / RETURN TO AUTHORITY. State which data class and activity the status covers. A harmless probe never silently clears real records.

Use claim, control, evidence, and unknown precisely

A claim states something about the arrangement: “requests are not retained.” A control names the mechanism and where it acts: “the primary request queue deletes the payload after response.” Evidence is a dated, applicable artifact—a diagram, contract section, configuration export, log schema, test, or audit. An unknown is a material question those artifacts do not answer.

Use five working states: ASSERTED for a party’s statement; DOCUMENTED for an applicable description; CONFIGURED for a recorded setting; OBSERVED for behavior visible in a bounded test; and UNKNOWN for an unanswered question. These are not a universal evidence ladder. None proves behavior outside its scope: configurations drift, observations miss hidden stores, and contracts do not reveal technical paths. Keep complementary evidence together.

The most dangerous words are compressed claims:

  • Private must name what is isolated, from whom, at which component, by which control.
  • Encrypted must name the data state and keys. Transit encryption does not cover plaintext inference or privileged access.
  • Zero retention must scope primary request stores, logs, safety records, caches, replicas, backups, and deletion propagation.
  • Customer controlled must name the credential, key, route, store, or policy the customer controls. Shared responsibility is not total control.
  • Not used for training is an organizational or contractual use claim. By itself, it says nothing about operational logging, support access, safety review, or retention.
  • Region locked must separately cover processing, storage, telemetry, backups, administrator and support access, subprocessors, and the organizations and jurisdictions involved.

Worked case: Cedar Ridge’s “private, zero-retention” endpoint

Cedar Ridge Water Operations proposes using a hosted model endpoint to draft maintenance after-action reports. A report may contain an equipment incident narrative, asset identifiers, a work-order excerpt, and technician names or contact details. The organization’s data owner classifies the real records as sensitive. The team has authority to evaluate with generated records but not to send real records until the endpoint review is complete.

The proposal packet supplies these facts:

  • An organization-managed report application sends the prompt and selected file to an organization API gateway. The gateway authenticates the user and injects a service credential from the organization secret store.
  • A gateway configuration export shows encrypted transport to the provider ingress. The gateway log schema contains user ID, request ID, destination, byte count, outcome, and a 30-day retention setting; it shows no prompt-content field.
  • The provider architecture record says the ingress routes the request to an inference worker, which processes plaintext in memory and returns a draft. It says the primary request queue deletes the payload after the response.
  • A sales sheet calls the service “private” and “zero retention.” A service appendix separately says safety events may be held for seven days. Whether that record can contain prompt content is not stated.
  • Central telemetry records tenant ID, request ID, token count, latency, and error code. Whether an error trace can include a content fragment is unknown.
  • The provider describes a break-glass support role. Approval, privilege duration, access logging, review, and customer notice are not supplied.
  • The proposal identifies the inference cluster as Region A. It does not identify telemetry, backup, support, or subprocessor locations. Backup scope and deletion propagation are unspecified.
  • The organization can disable the route and revoke its service credential. The provider controls the runtime and its administrator identities. A contract section says customer inputs and outputs are not used to train provider models.

Reveal: none of the five claims simply “fails first.” Each is underspecified. The operator’s first move is to scope it, not accuse the provider of violating it. Cedar Ridge can defend encrypted transport on the gateway-to-ingress crossing, customer revocation of the organization service credential, primary-queue deletion after response as described by the architecture record, and inference compute in Region A as described by the proposal. It cannot expand any of those into a whole-endpoint conclusion.

The disposition is CLEAR FOR SYNTHETIC PROBE for generated maintenance records, subject to the existing acceptable-use authority. It is simultaneously HOLD SENSITIVE USE until the telemetry content, safety-record scope, backup and deletion behavior, provider privilege controls, tenant isolation, and relevant locations and organizations have acceptable evidence and owners. If law or policy requires all processing and access to remain in an approved jurisdiction, the unresolved location row becomes STOP / RETURN TO AUTHORITY, not an engineering guess.

Inject the failure: mistake the gateway for the boundary

The team’s first diagram ends at the organization gateway. It labels the arrangement “local gateway; no egress; zero retention.” During an authorized synthetic probe, the gateway record shows an outbound request to the provider ingress, and the provider support view can find the request ID in central telemetry. The probe does not reveal whether content reached that telemetry. The observed records contradict the diagram’s completeness, not necessarily the provider’s scoped statements.

The failure is conceptual: the team treated the visible network hop as the service boundary, the customer’s inability to inspect provider logs as proof that none existed, and primary-queue deletion as total deletion. Recovery is to reopen all four planes, add provider ingress, inference, telemetry, support, safety records, and backups, replace blanket phrases with scoped rows, and preserve the content question as UNKNOWN. The team keeps real records on hold, assigns evidence requests, and reruns only the smallest permitted synthetic probe after the dossier names every expected egress and principal.

Practice: inspect an appliance that “runs in our cloud”

Valley Transit is considering a vendor-operated model appliance for summarizing generated maintenance notes. Use only these supplied facts:

  • The appliance compute and primary disk run in Valley Transit’s organization-managed cloud account in Region W. The application connects to a private address over an organization VPN.
  • A tenant credential is created through the vendor portal and stored in Valley Transit’s secret service. Valley Transit can revoke it. The vendor can issue a temporary support identity, but approval and audit details are absent.
  • A customer-managed key encrypts the primary disk. The appliance processes requests in plaintext memory. The key coverage for snapshots is documented; telemetry and runtime memory are outside that statement.
  • A vendor collector receives request ID, model version, latency, token count, and error class. An architecture slide says “no customer content,” but no schema, sample record, or test result is supplied.
  • Error records are documented as retained for 14 days. Nightly snapshots are retained for 30 days in the organization account. It is unknown whether a restored snapshot reintroduces an error record scheduled for deletion.
  • The vendor support role can restore a snapshot. Valley Transit cannot inspect the appliance’s provider-admin audit record in the supplied interface.
  • The contract says inputs and outputs are not used for vendor model training. Vendor support locations, subcontractors, and the legal organization operating the collector are not listed.
Predict before you map Is “runs in our cloud” enough to mark the arrangement local? Predict which locality—compute, persistence, administration, legal organization, or evidence—will break the single-word claim first.
  1. Draw all four planes. For every crossing, record data or credential, purpose, acting principal, transport, source owner, destination owner, and known egress.
  2. Write separate dossier rows for local, customer controlled, encrypted, no customer content leaves the account, and not used for training. Do not mark a row true or false until you have scoped it.
  3. For each row, name the control, supplied evidence, state, unresolved question, evidence owner, and decision effect.
  4. Issue one status for a generated-data probe and another for actual maintenance notes. Name the condition that changes each status.

Stop condition: do not send even generated data if credential privilege, intended egress, or authorization to probe is unresolved. Do not send real data while a required flow, retention path, administrator, location, or responsible owner remains unknown. Return credential and egress questions to security; data class and purpose to the data owner; retention and location to privacy, records, or legal authority; and provider evidence to the contract or vendor owner.

Reveal one defensible analysis
  • Local: compute and primary persistence are documented in the organization account and Region W. Administration is shared, telemetry crosses to a vendor collector, support organization and location are unknown, and evidence locality is weak because Valley Transit cannot inspect the provider-admin record. The unqualified claim is unsupported.
  • Customer controlled: Valley Transit controls its account, route, disk key, and tenant-credential revocation. The vendor controls software operation, support identity issuance, and the telemetry collector. Write split control, not total control.
  • Encrypted: primary disk and snapshots have documented key coverage. The private VPN path is named, but its transport protection is not supplied. The supplied facts say the appliance processes requests in plaintext memory; telemetry protection and privileged access remain unanswered. Scope the claim to the evidenced states.
  • No customer content leaves: the architecture slide is ASSERTED. The collector egress is known, but its exact schema and error behavior are not. Request the collector schema, a redacted sample record, change-control owner, and a bounded canary observation before relying on the claim.
  • Not used for training: the contract supports a scoped organizational-use claim. It does not settle telemetry, support access, retention, or restored snapshots.
  • Status: a generated-data probe may be cleared after authority, credential privilege, and expected collector egress are confirmed. Actual notes remain on hold until support access and audit, collector content and organization, retention after restore, subcontractors, and relevant location requirements are resolved.

Self-check: predict before you reveal

  1. A private network path and customer-owned gateway are documented. Can the dossier mark the endpoint private?
  2. Transport encryption and customer-managed storage keys are configured. What important access remains possible?
  3. A contract says inputs are not used for training. Which three questions does that statement not answer?
  4. A provider states that prompts are deleted after response. What stores must the retention row still inspect?
  5. A synthetic observation shows no content in the customer-visible log. What conclusion is defensible?
Reveal the self-check
  1. Only with a narrow scope, such as “the app-to-gateway path uses the private organization network.” Provider isolation, telemetry, administration, retention, and organizational boundaries remain separate.
  2. Authorized plaintext processing and privileged human or service access at runtime can remain. Key coverage also may exclude telemetry, memory, caches, or backups.
  3. Operational logging, retention or deletion, and administrator or support access. It also does not establish location.
  4. Primary request stores, queues, caches, safety or abuse logs, error traces, telemetry, replicas, snapshots, backups, archives, and restored copies.
  5. Only that the inspected record contained no content during that bounded observation. You have not proved that provider-internal records or other failure paths contain none.

Build a dossier before one real endpoint use

Select one proposed endpoint arrangement from your work. Keep the pass read-only and use no real sensitive data. Collect the applicable architecture diagram, data classification, service description, contract or policy terms, role matrix, log and telemetry schemas, retention schedule, backup and deletion description, subprocessor or organizational record, configuration exports, and any approved synthetic observations. Put the artifact version, date, applicability, and owner beside every claim.

Return to named people, not a vague “security review.” The data owner decides purpose and class. Security and identity owners decide egress, principals, credential privilege, and administrator controls. Privacy, records, or legal authorities decide retention, location, jurisdiction, and organizational requirements. The contract or vendor owner obtains provider evidence. If real data was sent accidentally, an unexpected destination appears, or a credential may be exposed, stop and follow the incident route.

Reopen the dossier when an endpoint version, route, provider, subprocessor, region, support model, log schema, retention term, backup process, key owner, administrator role, contract, or applicable requirement changes. Also reopen it when an observation conflicts with a documented claim. The NIST AI RMF Playbook recommends documenting third-party systems and regularly monitoring their risks and controls. A trust boundary is maintained evidence, not a diagram completed once.
