# Catchment Response: Technical Note

**Issued by:** Cordell Basin Hydrometric Service, Cordell Basin Relief Consortium
**Reference:** HYD-2026-009
**Issue:** 2
**Date of issue:** 19 May 2026
**Prepared by:** Lindqvist, H., Senior Hydrologist
**Distribution:** Basin gauge series subscribers; Consortium technical file

**Classification:** TRAINING SYNTHETIC - EXERCISE NORTHWIND SHELF. All units, organisations, people, places and events in this document are invented.

## 1. The question this note answers

Users routinely ask how long it takes for rain in the basin to show at a
particular station, and whether a rise seen at one station tells them what is
about to happen at another. The honest answer to the first question is that it
depends, and this note explains what it depends on. The answer to the second is
that it tells you something useful about direction and very little that is
reliable about size or timing, and the only way to know what a station is doing
is to read that station in the basin gauge series.

No timings or magnitudes are given here. They are not withheld out of caution;
they genuinely do not exist as fixed properties of the basin.

## 2. Overall character

The Cordell basin is a responsive catchment by temperament. The upper ground is
steep, the soils are thin over rock, and there is very little storage in the
system to absorb rain before it reaches a channel. Rain that falls on the western
moor block arrives in the Selwyn quickly and leaves quickly. The lower basin
behaves quite differently: the valley opens out, the gradient slackens, there is
floodplain to spread into, and the same volume of water passes as a lower, wider,
longer rise.

The practical effect is that the basin has no single response time. It has at
least two temperaments, and the boundary between them sits somewhere between the
Marrow Cut and the Ferry Point reach, moving depending on the event.

## 3. Why upper stations peak before lower ones

Four things act together, and it is worth separating them because they can be
confused.

**Travel time.** Water arriving at an upper station has to physically move
downstream to reach a lower one. This is the obvious component and the smallest
source of confusion. It is not constant: a flood wave moves faster in a full
channel than in an empty one, so the interval between an upper peak and a lower
peak is shorter in a large event than in a small one. The relationship runs the
opposite way to most people's intuition.

**Contributing area.** An upper station drains a small, uniform piece of ground
that gets wet all at once. A lower station drains everything above it, including
sub-catchments with different geology, different slope and different rainfall.
The lower station's rise is the sum of several arrivals that do not coincide, so
it is broader and later than any of them.

**Attenuation.** As the wave moves into wider, flatter reaches with floodplain
available, the peak spreads out. Volume is conserved; shape is not. A sharp rise
at the top of the catchment can become a long, gentle, unremarkable-looking rise
at the bottom, carrying the same water.

**Storm track.** This is the one that defeats simple rules. If rain falls on the
headwaters and moves downstream, the upper contribution and the lower
contribution arrive one after the other and the lower station sees a drawn-out
event. If the same rain moves upstream, the two contributions can arrive together
and the lower station sees a sharp one. Two events with identical rainfall totals
can produce quite different shapes at the lower stations depending only on which
way the weather was travelling.

## 4. Antecedent conditions

Everything above is modified by how wet the ground already is. On dry ground,
early rain is absorbed and the response is muted and late. On ground already
saturated, effectively all of the rain reaches a channel and the response is
immediate and large. The same rainfall over the same catchment can produce
responses at opposite ends of the range depending on what fell in the preceding
weeks.

This is why a rule of thumb built from one event fails on the next, and why the
Service does not publish one. It is also why the first substantial rain after a
dry spell tends to disappoint anyone expecting a rise, and the second tends to
surprise them.

## 5. The tributary station

COR-05, on the Halberd tributary, is the fastest thing in the network and the
least representative. Its catchment is tiny and its flume responds almost as soon
as rain reaches the estate ground. It is a good early indicator that something is
happening. It is a poor indicator of what will happen anywhere else, because its
catchment is not a scale model of the basin: it has different ground, different
cover and no floodplain at all.

## 6. What follows for users

A rise at an upper station is evidence that rain has fallen upstream. It is not a
forecast for a downstream station, and it should not be converted into one by
adding a remembered interval and a remembered increment. The interval is not
fixed, the increment is not fixed, and the relationship between them is not fixed
either.

Where a downstream condition matters, read the downstream station in the basin
gauge series. Where a future condition matters, read the forecast rows in the
same series, understanding what they are and how often they change; see
HYD-2026-015. Where a value has been relayed by a third party without its station
identifier and timestamp, treat it as hearsay and go back to the series.
