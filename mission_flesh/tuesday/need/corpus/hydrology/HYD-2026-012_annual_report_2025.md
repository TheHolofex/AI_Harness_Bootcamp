## 1. The shape of the year

2025 was, taken as a whole, an unremarkable year in the basin, which is worth
saying plainly because unremarkable years are the ones that establish what
remarkable means. Nothing occurred that required the Service to revise its
understanding of how the catchment behaves.

Winter opened wet. January and February ran with the ground already saturated
from the previous autumn, and successive frontal systems produced a run of
overlapping rises rather than distinct events. The lower stations spent much of
that period without returning to a settled baseline between rises, which is
normal for the season and is the main reason winter figures at Bastion Reach are
harder to interpret than figures from any other part of the year: backwater
influence is at its strongest when the Cordell is persistently high.

Spring was dry, and became progressively drier. From late March onward rainfall
was consistently below what the ground needed, and the basin moved into recession
early. The upper Selwyn recession through April and May was clean and
uninterrupted, which is unusual and made it a useful period for gauging work. The
Halberd tributary sat at the bottom of its range for long stretches from May
onwards and contributed nothing measurable to the Cordell for much of the summer.

Summer was low and stable, with two brief interruptions from convective rain in
July which produced sharp responses at SEL-01 and COR-05 and almost nothing
detectable at SEL-07. Those two events are a useful illustration of a point the
Service makes repeatedly: a substantial rise at the top of the catchment is not a
basin-wide event, and reading one station as a proxy for another will mislead in
both directions.

Autumn brought the seasonal rise, and this is discussed separately below.

The final quarter was wet without being exceptional. The basin returned to
saturated conditions during November and stayed there. December was mild and wet,
with no significant ice, and the year closed with the system running high and
responsive.

## 2. The autumn rise

The autumn rise is the most dependable feature of the basin's regime, and it is
routinely over-interpreted, so it is worth being precise about what is dependable
and what is not.

What is dependable is its occurrence. In every year of the basin record, the
transition from summer recession to autumn wet conditions has happened, and it
has happened within the autumn. Ground that has dried through the summer
re-wets, the soil moisture deficit closes, and thereafter rainfall converts to
runoff far more efficiently than it did a month earlier. This is a physical
process, not a coincidence of particular years, and it can be relied on to occur.

What is not dependable is anything else about it. The date on which the
transition begins varies widely between years, and it is not predictable from
conditions earlier in the year. The rate of the transition varies: in some years
the deficit closes over several weeks of moderate rain, in others it closes
within a single sustained wet spell and the change in the basin's behaviour is
abrupt. The magnitude varies most of all, and varies differently at different
stations, because it depends on which sub-catchments wet up first and on the
track of the systems that do the wetting.

The practical consequence is that the autumn rise is a reliable expectation and
an unreliable quantity. It is legitimate to plan on the basis that the basin will
become wetter and more responsive during the autumn. It is not legitimate to
carry a figure from a previous autumn, or from an average of previous autumns,
and treat it as an expectation for the current one. The Service has declined, and
will continue to decline, to publish a typical autumn value for any station,
because such a figure would be used as a prediction and it has no predictive
content.

Users needing a figure for any station, for any date in 2025 or for any other
period, should take it from the basin gauge series against the station and
timestamp concerned. No figures are reproduced in this report.

## 3. Network performance

Availability across the five stations was acceptable through the year. The
principal losses were the SEL-01 battery and regulator failure in May, the COR-05
card corruption in August, and the SEL-04 mounting displacement in November after
a timber strike at the pier head. Each is recorded with its infill treatment in
HYD-2026-004. The proportion of the year's rows carrying the estimated flag was
higher at COR-05 than at any other station, as it has been every year since
installation, and users of that record should read it accordingly.

Silting of the SEL-04 intake after high flows remains the most persistent
maintenance problem in the network and was addressed twice during the year
without a permanent fix. A revised intake design has been specified and awaits
call-off.

## 4. Rating and gauging

Fifty-one gaugings were made across the network. The dry spring gave good access
for low-flow work and the programme benefited; the corresponding weakness is that
the year added little to the upper end of any curve. The SEL-04 rating was revised
twice, in June and again in December after the section survey that followed the
pier head strike. COR-02 required no revision. SEL-07 remains on the loop rating
adopted in 2022 and its derived flow carries the widest uncertainty in the
network.

## 5. Staffing and programme

The Service ran with three established posts throughout the year and no
vacancies. The five-year benchmark re-levelling cycle is on programme, with
BM-FPT-1 and BM-NDD-1 due in 2027. Nothing in the year's work suggests a case for
altering the network's size or configuration.
