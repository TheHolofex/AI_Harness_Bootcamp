What this is worth at work

By Friday of a normal week, Monday's status document is wrong. Numbers moved, one blocker cleared, a new one landed — and somewhere a page still says otherwise. Most people handle that by asking a chat model to "summarize the latest" and getting a fresh essay every time: different structure, different emphasis, no way to tell what actually changed, no way to tell whether any of it is true.

This afternoon you build the other thing: a thinking machine that does the heavy lifting in knowledge work under your direction. One folder of source reports, one fixed format, a citation on every claim, and a saved path that regenerates the whole brief on command. When the world changes, you rerun it — and you can show why you trust the new brief, because you built the checks that catch it lying.

You will use two P1 chats. P1 — Direction & Closeout saves the release contract and final artifact-backed closeout. P1 — Daily Brief Build creates, refreshes, and repairs the brief. The saved source manifest, audit, and release record carry the work between chats and keep the decision readable a month from now.

  • A brief you can regenerate on command: script, skill (a packaged procedure the app can invoke on matching tasks), or saved one-command flow you can show, not a paste job
  • A risk-based citation audit you ran with your own eyes — five claims, pass/fail recorded, real findings repaired
  • An intact-corpus manifest check and a current-source refresh that changed only supported lines

The ideas doing the work

Five ideas carry this afternoon. Read them once now, and come back when a step surprises you — the reason will be one of these.

A thinking machine that does the heavy lifting

A judgment product is any recurring output someone acts on: a status brief, a risk rollup, a watch report, the Monday email your boss forwards upward. Ask a chat model for one and you get a draft — often a good draft. But the process that produced it is trapped inside one conversation. Tomorrow, when the facts move, you re-ask and re-explain, then receive a different essay whose changes you cannot account for.

A machine is the same product with its structure put where you control it: inputs pinned to a folder, format fixed in advance, a citation rule stated as law, and the run path written down — a script, a skill, or one saved command. The output structure stays fixed while evidence changes. That's what regenerable means.

The test is blunt: could you regenerate tomorrow's brief without re-explaining the mission from memory? Could a colleague, from what's on disk? If the answer is no, you don't own a machine — you own a transcript. That's also what going wrong looks like here: "regenerating" by re-prompting from memory, so each day's brief is a fork of the truth, and format drift that makes Tuesday's brief impossible to diff against Monday's.

Citation as a checkable circuit

Every factual claim in your brief carries a tag like [C3] that maps to a source file. Be precise about what the tag does: it does not make the claim true. Models write fluent claims whether or not the source holds them up. What the tag does is make the claim checkable — a wire you can follow from the sentence to the file, with your own eyes on both ends.

The eyes matter more than the wire. Ask the model "are your citations correct?" and it will grade its own work, usually generously — that's self-assessment, and it is worth nothing as evidence. Independent evidence means the reading happens outside the machine that wrote the claim: you open C3_qa_signoff.md and see whether it really says the EU export failed.

You can't walk every wire every day, so you sample by risk. Include the highest-consequence claims, qualified sources, and claims that merge evidence. A clean sample is a legitimate result; a failed row becomes a specific repair. Decorative citations, dropped qualifiers, and nonexistent source ids are the failures this audit is designed to catch.

Two source controls

The source-manifest preflight compares the intact input folder with the declared source set before any drafting begins. Missing, unexpected, or duplicate ids stop the run before a fluent brief can hide the mismatch.

The current-source refresh adds newly current material and reruns the same method. A healthy machine changes exactly where the evidence changed and holds unrelated sections steady. Together, these controls show what the run read and how the output responded without damaging the source set for a classroom test.

Acceptance criteria are the contract

Before the build, save five concrete fields: Outcome, Done looks like, Bounds, Evidence standard, and Stop / hand-back. P1 supplies the structure; you supply the real audience.

Specificity makes release possible. A daily brief for a named reader, with four fixed sections, stable citations, a manifest check, and one regenerate command can be checked line by line. “Keep me posted on status” cannot.

Draft versus judgment

The machine drafts; you judge. However good the draft, some calls in a status brief are yours and cannot be delegated: what gets printed as fact versus flagged as unverified, what "Blocked" actually means for this audience, what got left out, whether the thing goes out at all. The log line that separates the two — this is machine draft, this is my judgment — is how responsibility stays where it belongs.

The mechanism working against you here is comfort. The draft arrives fluent and complete — it reads like a finished thing, and editing a finished thing feels like damaging it. Fluency also masquerades as verification: a sentence that sounds checked raises none of the alarm a rough note would. So shipping the machine's framing whole is the path of least resistance, and every good draft that goes out untouched makes the next untouched one easier.

Here's the shape of a call that's yours. A source reports an outage it couldn't reproduce, and says so. Three moves exist: print it as established fact, print it with its status attached, or leave it out for this audience. The first launders a rumor — the caveat was the point. The choice between the other two depends on who's reading, and either way somebody decides. A model will pick one fluently; only you can own the pick, because when it's challenged, "that's what the AI said" is a defense that doesn't exist. The brief carries your name; the machine is an instrument you operate, and instruments don't sign things.

Release questions worth deciding

  • The model audited its own citations and reported all clear. What do you now know? Only that the instrument approves of itself. The audit exists because the reading has to come from outside the machine that wrote the claims.
  • How many claims do you audit before trusting the rest? Five is a starting point, not a law. The honest answer scales with the cost of a wrong claim reaching your audience — a brief that routes money or people earns a bigger sample.
  • Is printing "unverified: SSO outage reported, could not reproduce" better than omitting the rumor entirely? It depends on who's reading — an on-call engineer wants the rumor with its status attached; an exec summary may not. Either way somebody decides, and it's you.
  • The delta rerun changed a line you didn't expect. Bug or catch? Walk the wire before ruling. Sometimes the machine has surfaced a real implication you missed — and that's the machine earning its keep, not a defect.

The same machine on other desks

  • Ops / SRE: a nightly incident rollup regenerated from tickets and on-call notes. The manifest preflight catches the day an intake feed silently dies.
  • Program / PM desk: weekly status from a folder of team reports. The delta test is the late report that lands Friday at 4 p.m.
  • Intel / research: a watch brief over a source folder. The citation circuit is the difference between reporting and laundering rumor into fact.
  • Any desk with a boss: the Monday status email is already a judgment product. Audience, format, and citation rule are the same contract at smaller scale.

If you get stuck

  • Codex can't find mission_flesh. The folder exists on disk, but the app works from the project folder that's open — a path outside it won't resolve, and depending on your permission mode it may not be readable at all. Check which folder your project is opened at; open the course repo as the project, or copy mission_flesh/p1/ into it. Re-cloning fixes a different problem.
  • The build keeps sprawling. Re-read the LIVE contract, then narrow the data scope — fewer sections or files — while keeping the manifest, citations, audit, and release checks.
  • Citations won't stick. The model tags loosely or invents. Don't fix tags by hand — that converts machine error into your error. Reject the draft and restate the rule as an accept check: unresolvable tags fail the run.
  • The preflight does not match the intact folder. Compare SOURCE_MANIFEST.md with C1–C6 and the held-back patch. Resolve the mismatch before drafting; record HOLD if time runs out.
