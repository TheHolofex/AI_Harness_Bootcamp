Why this desk exists

Somewhere in a normal work week, a model drafts something you pass upward — a status rollup, an extraction from documents you didn't have time to read twice, a summary that becomes someone else's operating picture. When that draft is wrong, the error arrives wearing your name. The trap is quiet: a model's fluent answer feels like a second opinion because it's phrased like one. It isn't. It's one witness, interviewed once.

The countermeasure is a desk, and you build it now: the same extraction demand, run verbatim through two engines that share neither product nor model family, a comparator that surfaces every place they differ, and a ruling — from you, on evidence — for each one. An engine here means a model running inside a harness product; today's two are the Codex app and OpenCode running Grok on your xAI key. By the end you own the whole circuit, and it reruns on Monday's real work with nothing but a new corpus.

What you will leave with

  • BRIEF-v1 run unchanged on the Codex app and OpenCode — two raw outputs on disk you can point to
  • Run identity declared for both engines — model id, version, folder, and the context each one carried
  • A filled comparator with ≥3 material disagreements, or written proof you searched for them
  • At least one model-asserted claim killed on non-model evidence — and the kill test in your hands as a repeatable move
  • An adjudication note a supervisor could act on cold
  • The habit set: single-engine ban on consequential claims, comparator over vibes, kills that name their evidence
  • Stretch: Many Minds — a baseline, a subagent synthesis, and a delta sheet that defends whether parallel actually helped

Before you start

The craft under the desk

Read these once before you start, then come back to whichever idea a disagreement — or a suspicious agreement — puts to work.

Agreement is not truth; disagreement is a sensor

When two engines return the same answer, it's tempting to call the answer confirmed. What a match actually establishes is narrower: two systems produced the same output. If their failure modes overlap, they can match for shared-wrong reasons — and their failure modes do overlap. Both models trained on overlapping public text, both read the exact corpus you handed them, and both are optimized to produce fluent, confident prose. Where the corpus is thin or ambiguous, each fills the gap with the most plausible completion — and the most plausible completion is often the same wrong guess. A match therefore tells you a claim is either well-supported or a shared blind spot, and it cannot tell you which. That's why matched claims still get sample-checked.

Disagreement is the more useful signal, and it's worth being precise about what it is. Two different model families under two different harnesses diverge mostly where the evidence underdetermines the answer — a contradiction between sources, a gap the brief doesn't cover, a synonym the status vocabulary maps two ways. So each disagreement row is a pointer to exactly the claims where mechanical processing ran out and judgment has to take over. That is what the second engine buys for its cost: not a better answer, but a map of where your attention belongs. An operator who reads the comparator top to bottom spends twenty minutes; an operator who reads only the disagreement rows spends five and misses nothing the engines could have caught — the shared blind spots remain, and they are exactly what the sample-check on matched claims is for.

The failure smell on this idea has a name in the pass bars: “they basically agreed.” If that sentence appears without a table behind it, no comparison happened — an impression happened. The other failure is subtler: treating every split as proof one engine is defective. Usually neither is defective; the evidence is genuinely thin, and the honest ruling is UNKNOWN with a note saying why.

Independence hygiene — the cargo neither engine declares

A two-engine comparison is only evidence if both engines got the same job with nothing extra riding along. Neither engine helps you with this. The Codex app loads AGENTS.md from whatever folder you open as the project — including the one you hot-rodded in P2 — plus %USERPROFILE%\.codex\AGENTS.md, any skills it discovers, and memories if you enabled them. OpenCode, for its part, reads %USERPROFILE%\.claude\CLAUDE.md and .claude\skills unless OPENCODE_DISABLE_CLAUDE_CODE=1 is set — pre-work set it, and today is the day that setting earns its keep. Neither product announces any of this at run time. The cargo is invisible until you go looking.

Here is what going wrong looks like, because it's the most common way this desk fails: you run the brief inside your P2 project, the Codex output comes back noticeably sharper, and you conclude the Codex app is the stronger engine. It wasn't the engine — it was your own instruction file riding along on one side. One engine plus your notes is not two engines. And the error cuts both ways: an advantage you didn't declare looks exactly like a capability you don't have. Every conclusion you draw from a contaminated delta is a finding about your setup wearing a costume.

The discipline is declare or clear, and either is honest — silence is the only wrong answer. Clear means a fresh folder holding only the frozen corpus, the disable flag set (OPENCODE_DISABLE_CLAUDE_CODE=1), and --pure on the OpenCode run so installed plugins stay out of the path. Declare means writing the run identity down like a lab notebook entry: engine, model id, version, folder, and which instruction files, skills, and memories were live. The comparator's first table exists for exactly this, and it gets filled before any side-by-side row. At work this habit generalizes far past today's two products: any time you compare AI outputs — models, prompts, vendors, versions — the first question is what each side was carrying.

The frozen brief is the control

You're about to attribute output differences to the engines. That attribution is only valid if everything else is identical — the brief byte for byte, the corpus files, the join rule. In experiment terms the brief is the control: the thing held fixed so the remaining variation means something. This is why BRIEF-v1 is frozen and why editing it mid-run is the one move that ruins the afternoon quietly. A clarification you give one engine and not the other converts your comparison into a story about your prompting.

Read the brief once as a piece of engineering and you'll see each rule closing a degree of freedom. WatchIDs must come from watchlist.csv — so the join is deterministic and no model gets to guess which entity a paragraph is about. Status must be one of four values — so synonyms can't blur into unfalsifiable prose. Every factual cell needs a quote of twenty words or fewer — so checking a claim takes seconds, not minutes. Contradictions are kept, not resolved — so the model can't silently pick a side of a dispute that's yours to rule on. Unlisted entities are fenced into their own section — so scope creep is visible instead of woven in. Remove any of these and the comparator fills with noise you can't adjudicate.

An underspecified brief fails differently, and the failure is instructive: both engines drift, but in different directions, and the disagreement count explodes. When that happens the divergence is telling you about the brief, not the engines — which is why a flood of disagreements is a diagnostic, not a jackpot. If you find yourself wanting to tighten the wording, the honest path is a labeled variant: BRIEF-v2, run as a stretch experiment on both engines, with the disagreement-rate change recorded. That experiment — v1 versus v2, measured — is one of the sharpest things you can bring back to any prompt-writing argument at work.

Adjudication is a ruling, not an average

When the engines split on a field, three tempting moves are all wrong: ask a third model to break the tie, keep the answer that sounds more confident, or split the difference. All three keep the decision inside model-land, where the error was born. The correct move is shorter than any of them — open the source file. The brief's quote rule means each engine has told you where it thinks its evidence lives; go there and read. Then rule the field: which side wins, why, and what evidence outside either model settles it. Write the ruling in the verdict column. If the sources genuinely don't settle it, UNKNOWN is a ruling too — often the most honest one on the sheet.

For claims that resist a quick source check — especially claims both engines agree on — there's a sharper instrument: the kill test. Copy the corpus, delete the file a claim cites, and rerun the brief — the stale-source test from P1's brief machine, re-aimed at a single claim instead of the whole machine. A grounded claim dies with its evidence: the row goes UNKNOWN or the field empties. A claim that survives losing its own source was never resting on it — the model was confabulating, generating a plausible assertion and decorating it with a citation after the fact. That's a kill, and it's the strongest kind, because you demonstrated the failure instead of suspecting it. One earned kill teaches you more about a model's evidence discipline than fifty smooth successes.

Adjudication done this way produces a specific artifact: a note a supervisor can act on cold, with every ruling walkable back to a file and a line. That's the difference between “the AI said” and intelligence work. On a real desk the rulings are what you're paid for — the engines are interchangeable; the verdict column is you.

Three instruments that look alike

Once you've run a second engine, it's easy to blur it together with two neighbors that look similar and aren't. A second engine is a different product and model family given the same frozen job — it buys independence, and it's the only one of the three that can catch a shared-vendor blind spot. A subagent is a specialist the Codex app spawns under your commander chat — same product, same model family, so it buys division of labor and clean context, never independence. A second human brings actual judgment and accountability — the one thing neither machine instrument carries, which is why a human challenger stays on your transfer list even after this desk works.

InstrumentWhat it buysWhat it cannot do
Second engine (OpenCode)Independent failure modes; a real check on the first engineOwn the verdict; guarantee truth when it agrees
Subagent (Codex app)Parallel specialist lenses; keeps the main chat cleanProvide independence — same model family, same cargo
Second humanJudgment and accountability; a challenge that doesn't share your incentivesScale to every claim; be available on demand for every run

Here is what mistaking one for another looks like: three subagents converge on a finding and the report upstairs calls it “independently confirmed.” It is nothing of the kind — same product, same model family, same cargo; the agreement was manufactured, not found. The Many Minds stretch puts the middle row in your hands: one commander, three parallel lenses, and a measured answer to whether parallel helped. Whatever mix of instruments you run, the merges and the kills stay with the commander — the sentence to be able to say cold is subagent ≠ second engine ≠ second human.

Run of exercise

Six stages, in order. Check each item when its proof exists on disk, not when you've read it. Progress saves in this browser — the same tick shows on the home page.

Stage 01 · Frame the run

Before either engine sees the brief, you and the AI agree on what winning looks like. Five minutes of dialogue here saves an hour of drift later.

  1. Open chat Operator — Direction & Log and paste the brief starter from operator/DIRECTION_BRIEF.md. Fill all five fields: Outcome, Done looks like, Bounds, Evidence standard, Stop/hand-back. The outcome here is two named artifacts — a filled comparator and an adjudication note someone could read cold; “compare the models” is not an outcome, and the AI should challenge it. Status must read LIVE before you touch either engine. If the dialogue passes eight minutes, accept the wording you have and move.

Stage 02 · Load the frozen brief

Now you set up the control: the brief read as a contract, and a working folder where nothing rides along that you haven't declared. What you should see at the end of this stage is a folder holding exactly five files — the brief and your track's four corpus files — and nothing else.

  1. Open instruments/p3_frozen_brief/BRIEF-v1.md and read it as a contract. Notice what each rule pins down: WatchIDs come from watchlist.csv, status has four allowed values, every factual cell needs a quote of twenty words or fewer, contradictions are kept rather than resolved, off-watchlist entities are fenced into Unlisted mentions. Do not edit a word — the moment the text drifts, the comparison stops being about the engines. If a rule seems ambiguous, write the ambiguity down; it's comparator material later.
  2. Make a clean working folder and copy in BRIEF-v1.md plus your track's four files from instruments/p3_frozen_brief/engineering/corpus/ (watchlist.csv, F1_status_page.md, F2_oncall_notes.md, F3_deploy_log.md) or instruments/p3_frozen_brief/mission_ops/corpus/ (watchlist.csv, F1_ops_log.md, F2_field_hawk.md, F3_drone_snip.md). Same track as P2. Both engines read this folder and nothing else; joins use watchlist.csv IDs only — models do not invent join keys. Running in place instead is allowed, but then every live instruction file gets declared in the comparator.

Settle what each engine is carrying before either one runs

Both load context you never typed and neither announces it: the Codex app picks up AGENTS.md, skills, and memories from wherever you launch it — including the harness you hot-rodded in P2 — and OpenCode reads %USERPROFILE%\.claude\CLAUDE.md and .claude\skills unless OPENCODE_DISABLE_CLAUDE_CODE=1 is set. Run from a clean folder holding only the frozen corpus, or write down exactly what was live on each side. One engine plus your notes is not two engines. An advantage you didn't declare looks exactly like a capability you don't have — if the two sides ran with different context, that is a finding about your setup, not a disagreement between models.

Stage 03 · Dispatch both engines

The same brief, verbatim, to each engine in turn. What you should see from each: the four-part shape the brief demands — table, contradictions, unlisted mentions, gaps. What you must not do between the two runs: explain, clarify, or coach. The brief does the talking; you take dictation and save files.

  1. Open the clean folder as a project in the Codex app, start a build chat, and give it BRIEF-v1 exactly — point it at the file or paste the text unaltered. Copy the full response into a new file output_codex.md yourself — Notepad is fine; don't ask the engine to write its own transcript. Record the model from the picker beneath the composer and the app version from Settings. You should see a table with one row per WatchID, then contradictions, unlisted mentions, gaps; if you get prose instead, rerun the same verbatim brief once — coaching one engine breaks the control.
  2. Same brief, same corpus, second engine. In the terminal you'll run from, confirm $env:OPENCODE_DISABLE_CLAUDE_CODE prints 1 (pre-work set it). Then, from the clean folder: opencode run --pure -m "xai/$env:HB_XAI_MODEL" "Read BRIEF-v1.md in this folder and follow it exactly." — --pure keeps plugins out; -m pins the Grok id staff posted rather than inheriting whichever model ran last. Copy the response into a new output_opencode.md yourself, and record the -m value plus opencode --version beside it. An error mentioning 429 or quota is usually a capped key — flag staff instead of reinstalling.

Stage 04 · Compare on paper

Two outputs exist; now the desk earns its name. Identity first, then the side-by-side. A material disagreement changes an operational fact — a status flip, one engine asserting where the other says UNKNOWN, different evidence behind the same cell. A wording difference that changes no fact is cosmetic, and cosmetic rows are how comparators eat an hour.

  1. Open instruments/p3_frozen_brief/COMPARATOR.md. Fill the run-identity table before any side-by-side row: engine, model id, version, folder, and which instruction files, skills, or memories were live on each side. Then work field by field, one row per material disagreement, and count them. Three or more: on to adjudication. Fewer: document where you searched and why the task separated so little — that written proof of search meets the bar honestly.

Stage 05 · Adjudicate, kill, and write the note

Every disagreement row gets a ruling from the source file — which side, why, what evidence outside either model. Matched claims get sample-checked. And at least one asserted claim dies today, on evidence you can show. If nothing on the sheet deserves killing, run the kill test on the strongest claim both engines agree on and watch what happens.

  1. Find a claim a model asserted that you can discard on evidence outside either model — the source line says otherwise, the watchlist has no such ID, or the kill test settles it: copy the corpus, delete the cited file, rerun, and watch whether the claim survives losing its own evidence. In the comparator, quote the assert and write the discard reason. “It seemed weak” is not a kill; a kill names its evidence.
  2. Write the note at the bottom of the comparator: the operational picture as you now rule it — what is true, what is UNKNOWN and why, what you killed and on what evidence. This note is the run's final watch product, the artifact your log points at. Test it by imagination: someone who has read nothing else picks this up during a handover. If they'd have to ask you a question before acting on it, it isn't finished. A supervisor-readable note is measured in clarity, not length.

Stage 06 · Bank what transfers

One minute of naming what transfers is what turns today's run into something you reach for on a real desk.

  1. Put the three portable habits in your own words before the pulse: no single-engine answer on consequential claims; a kill names non-model evidence; the comparator table beats “they basically agreed.” Then name the first real decision at work you'd run through this desk — that's the seed the transfer chat will grow.

Questions worth arguing about

  • If the engines agree on every field, what did the second engine buy you? One answer: nothing, this run — cancel it. A better answer: the proof of search, the sample-checks, and the standing ability to catch the day they don't agree. Insurance you never claim on still priced the risk. Where's the line for your desk?
  • Which is worse: a wrong claim both engines agreed on, or a disagreement you ruled incorrectly? The first fails silently and scales; the second leaves a trail — your ruling, your reasoning, reviewable. What does that say about where checking effort should go?
  • When is a third engine worth it? Each added engine buys diminishing independence and linearly growing adjudication cost. The comparator has an optional third-output slot — argue for a claim class where you'd actually pay that cost.
  • What separates the kill test from simply distrusting the model? One is a falsifiable procedure with an observable outcome; the other is a mood. What else on your desk deserves the same upgrade from suspicion to procedure?

The same desk at work

  • Incident and status rollups — two engines extract service status from the status page, on-call notes, and deploy log; the comparator catches the automated bot claiming clear while a human still shows degraded. The engineering corpus is this desk in miniature.
  • Field and watch reporting — clearance times, movement claims, single-source rumors: adjudicated against the raw logs before anything reaches a leadership brief. The mission_ops corpus is this desk in miniature.
  • Contract and compliance review — two engines extract obligations and deadlines from the same agreement; disagreement rows go back to the clause text, and the ruling cites page and line.
  • Vendor and hiring diligence — claims extracted from proposals or CVs, run twice; anything that fails a kill test never reaches the decision memo.

Stretch lane · Many Minds, one commander

After the twin-engine floor is met, a different kind of “many”: not two vendors, but several specialists under one commander — you — with a measured answer to whether parallel helped. A subagent is a specialist the Codex app starts for one bounded job; it works off to the side and returns a summary to your chat. The mastery path is three artifacts in order: a baseline single-chat review with no subagents, then three parallel lenses (correctness, test gaps, spec drift) merged with visible dedupe and at least one corpus-evidenced kill, then a delta sheet that defends a verdict — improved, duplicated, or noisy. Skipping the baseline turns the stretch into a product demo: without it you cannot say what parallel added.

Two details worth noticing while you run it. The three-subagent cap lives in your prompt wording, which makes it procedure-enforced — it holds because the model cooperates and you're watching. The app also has a real setting, agents.max_concurrent_threads_per_session, which holds whether the prompt cooperates or not — tool-enforced. Feel the difference: a procedure-enforced bound is a promise, and a tool-enforced bound is a wall. And if two of your specialists ever needed to write at once, a worktree — a second checkout of the same repository — is the isolation instrument; since nothing in this run writes to the repo, the worktree proof stays the optional deeper cut. Full walkthrough: mission_flesh/p3/MANY_MINDS.md; pack: instruments/p3_multi_agent/; the four stretch checks live on the mastery floor below.

Operator pulse — required every block

The same six-beat ritual closes every half-day: it is how a mission becomes evidence. Paste-ready prompts →

  1. Twin-engine work ran in build chats, not in Operator — Direction & Log. The operator chat holds judgment; build chats hold the mess. If they mixed today, note it in the log rather than rewriting history — the trail is the point.
  2. Back in Direction & Log: what you directed, mid-run changes, verdict (ACCEPT / REJECT / ACCEPT WITH FIXES), and evidence pointers to output_codex.md, output_opencode.md, and the comparator. Write what judgment you still own — for this desk, that is every ruling in the adjudication note.
  3. Against P3's MVP list: met / not yet, each with an evidence pointer. No inflation. The two bars most often claimed early here: three disagreements (were they material?) and the kill (was it earned, or polite?).
  4. Chat Adversarial — P3: frozen prompt + artifacts + provisional verdict. Expect attacks on undeclared cargo, cosmetic rows counted as material, and a cheap kill. Copy the final line into your log: ADVERSARIAL: stood | wounded | failed. Fix if wounded.
  5. Ritual 0–3 · mission HIT/PARTIAL/MISS · quality STRONG/ADEQUATE/WEAK · time to result (minutes). P3 carries a deep mark: Comparator on BRIEF-v1: disagreements n · kills n · verdict — counts and ruling, one line. What the disagreement rate taught you can ride along as an optional one-sentence note beside the mark.
  6. Chat Operator — Transfer 30-60-90: paste the every-session prompt. A session-log row is required even if the seed is small today — and P3's usually isn't: the single-engine ban and the comparator habit go on the 30-day list with a named work claim to run first.

Mastery floor — evidence must survive adversarial review

Mark met / not yet against your own artifacts, not your memory of them. If the adversarial pass wounds an item, uncheck it until it is fixed. Full bar text: operator/PASS_BARS.md · P3.

The floor

  1. Evidence looks like: output_codex.md and output_opencode.md on disk, produced from byte-identical brief text with no coaching between runs. If you clarified anything for one engine only, this is not yet met — rerun that side.
  2. Evidence looks like: every WatchID row in both outputs matches an ID in watchlist.csv, with off-watchlist entities fenced into Unlisted mentions. A model that joined on a display name instead of an ID fails this bar even when the answer happens to be right.
  3. Evidence looks like: COMPARATOR.md with the run-identity table filled for both engines and side-by-side rows carrying keep / discard / unknown verdicts. “They basically agreed” with no table behind it is the named not-yet.
  4. Evidence looks like: at least three rows where the engines differ on an operational fact — or a written proof of search naming the fields you checked and why separation was low. Cosmetic wording differences count toward neither.
  5. Evidence looks like: the model's assert quoted, plus a discard reason grounded outside either model — a source line, a missing watchlist ID, or a kill-test result. One earned kill beats three polite ones.
  6. Evidence looks like: the note reads cold. Someone who saw none of the run can state what is operationally true, what is unknown, and what you killed — without asking you anything.
  7. Evidence looks like: the Operator Log names brief id BRIEF-v1 and points at both raw outputs, the comparator, and the final watch product — the adjudication note at the bottom of the comparator. Two runs you can't identify are not evidence.

Pass bar (mastery floor)

MVP is not “it ran.” Evidence must survive adversarial review — and the named not-yets for this desk are one engine only, “they basically agreed,” and a verdict without a comparator. Full list: operator/PASS_BARS.md · section P3.

If you get stuck

  • OpenCode won't run the brief. Read the error body before touching the install: 429 or quota means a capped key wearing an auth-failure costume, and a brand-new key does nothing until its ACLs attach. Both are staff fixes. While you wait, keep working the Codex side and prep the comparator.
  • The engines agree on everything. That's not a failed run. First confirm both sides ran clean — shared cargo manufactures agreement. Then document the search, sample-check the strongest matches, and run one kill test; the floor accepts proof of search in place of three rows.
  • The comparator is eating the block. You're logging cosmetic rows. Narrow the data scope — fewer fields, material disagreements only — and never the judgment standard. If a concept is what's blocking you, ask the lead to diagram it live, then continue on your own chair.
