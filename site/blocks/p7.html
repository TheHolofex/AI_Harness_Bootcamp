Start here

All week, the agents could change the next step while they ran. That helps when you do not know the path yet. A lot of desk work is not like that. The same kind of decision repeats, in the same shape, many times: code invoices, route tickets, fold field notes into one sheet.

For that work, a free agent is the wrong tool. You get many different paths. You cannot check them easily. Small drift is hard to see. A better tool is a workflow. You design the steps once. Every row takes the same stations. An AI step fills one station. Simple checks test its output. A hold for you stops doubtful rows. Only clear rows reach the master sheet.

Today you build that workflow in n8n. Then you sort real work from your desk into three bins: free agent, workflow with a hold, or keep it human.

Learn the ideas before you build

Five ideas carry the afternoon. Read them once. Come back when a step needs the reason.

1. Two tools, one choice

A free agent picks its next move while it runs. The model reads what just happened and chooses the next step inside your limits. A workflow uses a path you designed once. The path does not change from row to row. That is the whole difference.

With a free agent, the model owns the path and you own the limits. With a workflow, you own the path and the model fills one station you built for it.

This morning asked how to bound an agent that keeps working on its own. This afternoon asks a higher question. Should this work use a free agent at all, or will a workflow with one AI station finish it more reliably?

The wrong choice costs you either way. A free agent on five hundred near-identical rows gives you five hundred different paths. Slow. Costly. Hard to check. A workflow on true judgment work forces odd cases through a shape that does not fit. The person who reviews then becomes a rubber stamp.

Ask two questions of any workload. What changes from item to item? What does a wrong answer cost? High volume with the same shape points at a workflow. High change from item to item points at a free agent. High cost of error points at a hold for you, or at keeping the work human.

2. One pass. No mid-run fix.

Nothing in the workflow you build re-reads its own output and revises. Each node runs once per item and hands the result forward. There is no "look again and fix it" step inside the workflow. That is why the workflow is cheap, fast, and easy to predict.

So judgment must go in early. You put it in the path design and in where the hold sits. The AI step will not push back the way a chat agent does. Give it bad input and it processes bad input with confidence. The flow moves on. There is no next message. Safety must be built in.

For any automation idea at work, ask: where does this path correct itself? If the answer is nowhere, the path design and the hold carry all the judgment. Give those two things most of the review time.

3. The hold for you is a design choice

A hold is a point where the flow stops and a person decides. Two things make a hold real.

First, place. A hold before the master sheet means bad rows never land. A hold after the write means the error is already the record when you see it.

Second, evidence. The hold must arrive with the row, the AI output, and the reason it held. A bare yes/no prompt trains you to click yes. The hold rate matters too. If everything holds, people stop reading. If nothing holds, you only have a drawing of a hold. Aim for holds that deserve attention.

When someone asks you to "review" automated output, ask where the review sits relative to the write. Before the write, you are a quality check. After the write, you are a stamp with a login.

4. Do not trust the AI step. Check it.

Checks are simple tests the flow runs on the AI output before anything lands. Required fields present. Values from an allowed set. Numbers in range. Output in the shape you asked for. The model returns text. The flow turns text into fields. Later nodes trust those fields. That handoff is where workflows break. Put the checks there. If a check fails, hold the row. Never send a failed row to the sheet.

"Looks reasonable" is not a rule. "Region is one of EU, US, GLOBAL" is a rule.

One concrete move today: rows.csv has an expected_gate column. That is your answer key. Drop that column before the AI step sees a row. Never let the system under test see its own answer key.

5. The sort you keep

The lasting skill is not wiring n8n nodes. It is the sort. Take any workload from your desk and place it in one of three bins: free agent, workflow with a hold, or keep it human.

Use the same two questions: what changes from item to item, and what a wrong answer costs. Volume decides whether a workflow is worth building. The keep-it-human bin has its own test. When the verdict is the work product — a hiring call, a trust call, an accept or reject people trust because a person made it — automating it removes the thing that made it valuable.

Write the sort down, per workload, with reasons. n8n will change. The model will change. The sorted list and the two questions transfer.

Work questions

  • If the hold stops 40% of rows, is the AI step earning its place? At what hold rate would a keyword rule do the same job for free, and what evidence would settle it?
  • When does review become a rubber stamp? Check hold place, hold rate, and whether rejected rows change anything upstream.
  • When volume grows ten times, what breaks first — the checks, the hold, or you? The honest answer usually reshapes where the hold should sit.
  • Should a free agent be allowed to build the workflow that replaces it? You directed an adaptive agent all week. Today you hard-coded one path. Who should write the checks that limit the AI step, and why not the model itself?

The same workflow on other desks

  • Finance operations: invoices in, AI extracts vendor, amount, and cost code. Checks enforce vendor allow-list and amount ranges. The hold stops out-of-policy items with the extracted fields attached.
  • Support lead: tickets in, AI routes by product and severity. Checks catch legal-threat and security keywords no matter what the model decided. The hold always stops those for a person.
  • Watch desk: field reports sorted into a master picture. A confident false all-clear is exactly why the hold exists.
  • Field operations: daily site reports folded into a rollup. The hold stops anything that claims completion of work another report contradicts.

If you get stuck

  • The AI step will not behave. Test it alone on one row and read its raw output before you touch the rest of the workflow. Prose instead of your format means the prompt never pinned the shape. An auth-looking failure with 429 or quota in it means a capped key. Flag it rather than reinstalling.
  • The hold stops everything, or nothing. Read one run end to end. Fix parsing before thresholds. Confirm the hold sits before the write. Both failure modes usually live in those two places.
  • The write-up feels like vibes. Let the two questions do the forcing — what changes from item to item, and what a wrong answer costs — then write the answers into the template why columns. If you cannot name either for a workload, that workload stays human. That is itself a finding.
  • Narrow the data scope, never the judgment standard. Three rows through a workflow with a hold beat eight through a workflow with none.
  • Ask the lead to diagram the one concept blocking you, then continue on your own chair.
