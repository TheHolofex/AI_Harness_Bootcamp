Prose you must trust, or links you can walk

Ask a chatbot a hard question and you get prose. It may be right, it may be confident and wrong, and either way the answer dies in a scrollback you'll never search again. Today you build the alternative: an assistant working inside a vault — Obsidian's name for a folder of plain-text notes that link to each other, displayed as a web you can navigate. When the machine works inside your structure, every claim in its answer can point at a note you can open. The difference between the two is not intelligence. It is the trail.

The scenario is close to most real desks. A director needs a straight picture of export risk versus SSO stability before Thursday's steering meeting, and the raw material is four messy captures sitting in an inbox: a Slack thread, an on-call paste, an undecided ship-order question, and the director's own ask. The facts exist, scattered, and the question spans all of them. You will turn the scatter into a system that answers — and then hold that system to a standard chat never meets: citations you personally walked.

What you will leave with

  • A vault that answers director-level questions with citations you can click, walk, and verify — plus the audit habit that keeps it honest
  • A triage method that turns raw captures into titled, linked notes a machine can navigate
  • The scope craft: you can bound an assistant's reach to exactly what you can audit, in the project and in the brief
  • A Morning Brief note you can regenerate Monday at work, inside your own system
  • A written verdict trail: which answers stood, which you revised or killed, and why

How a second brain actually works

Five ideas make a second brain trustworthy, and every one of them outlives this particular app.

Your structure, the machine's reach

A note in the vault is an ordinary Markdown file on disk. A wikilink — [[Note Name]] written inside a note — is plain text that Obsidian turns into a clickable connection to the note with that title. Nothing is hidden in a database: the assistant and Obsidian are looking at the same files, so when the assistant writes or links a note, it appears in Obsidian immediately. That shared ground is the whole trick. An answer built from your files can cite your files, and a citation to a file is something you can open.

Compare that with asking the same question in a bare chat. The model answers from whatever it absorbed in training plus whatever you pasted, the reasoning is invisible, and next month the exchange is unfindable. The chat is a workroom; the vault is the record. What goes wrong when people skip this distinction is a familiar graveyard: months of genuinely good AI conversations trapped in scrollback, none of it linked, none of it citable, all of it dead the day the chat history is cleared. At work, the rule that follows is simple — anything that must outlive the conversation gets landed in the structure before the conversation ends.

Scoped access is knowledge governance

In the Codex app, a project is a folder you open — and that folder is the assistant's working world. Open the vault folder as the project and the vault is what the assistant can reach; your tax returns, your other clients, and your credentials are not in the folder, so they are not in play. That is the structural layer. Your Direction Brief adds a written layer on top: what the assistant may read, what it may write, and what must never leave the vault — names, incident details, anything you would not paste into a status email.

Two layers, two different guarantees. The folder edge governs what can happen; the brief governs what may happen, and it only binds because you check it. The classic failure is opening the project one level too high "to be safe" — now the assistant's reach includes everything in your user folder, and you can no longer say what informed an answer. The craft here is the same one used to scope any aide's clearance: enough access to be useful, and no more than you can audit. It is worth pausing on how rare this move is — most people either lock the machine out of everything or open everything to it. Operators scope.

Retrieval is agentic search over your links

The assistant hasn't memorized your vault. When you ask a cross-vault question, it searches for likely files, opens them, and follows wikilinks from note to note — the same walk you would do by clicking, done at machine speed. That style of retrieval is called agentic search: the machine navigates and reads rather than matching keywords once. The practical consequence is easy to miss and changes everything about how you organize: folders alone are closets, links are roads. A question about export risk finds EU-Export-v2 because your Systems hub links to it and the Slack-thread note links to it — not because it sits in a well-named folder.

This is why triage quality decides answer quality before any question is asked. A vault of two hundred orphan notes with no links gives the machine nothing to walk, and cross-vault answers come back thin — or worse, confidently padded with invention where the roads ran out. A seeded hub note like MOC.md (a map of content — a note whose job is to link out to everything important) is the trailhead the machine starts from. At work, the ontology you would hand a new analyst on day one — people, systems, events, decisions, or whatever fits your desk — is exactly the map the machine navigates. Build it for the human and the machine gets it free.

A citation you verify by navigation

A note-link citation is a claim plus a door. The audit discipline is the one you built Monday — check citations with your own eyes, never by asking the model whether it is sure, which measures confidence, not truth — with the cite upgraded from a file tag to a link you can walk. The move has four beats: click the link, read the note, find the sentence that actually carries the claim, and mark a verdict in writing: support, partial, or not supported. Partial matters more than it sounds — a note that says a rumor is unreproduced only partially supports an answer that lists it as a live risk, and partial counts as failed when the answer stated the claim as fact.

What going wrong looks like: a citation to a note that does not exist (Obsidian renders those links faded, and clicking one offers to create an empty note instead of showing evidence), or a citation to a real note that never says what the answer claims. Plausible, cited, and wrong is the most dangerous combination in AI-assisted work, because the citation costume defeats the skepticism a bare claim would trigger. The only tell for an unwalked citation is that you never walked it. At work this becomes the standard you demand of any AI-produced brief, including ones other people hand you: every claim someone will act on carries a trail a skeptic can walk in under a minute. An answer that spans many notes with walkable links is a different product from a summary — a summary asks for trust; a trailed answer offers inspection.

The Morning Brief: a knowledge product that regenerates

On Monday you built a machine that makes the answer instead of a one-shot answer. The Morning Brief is that same pattern moved inside your own knowledge system: a dated note the assistant regenerates from the current state of the vault, every claim citing the note it stands on. Because the brief is generated from notes, it inherits the audit trail — and because the knowledge lives in the notes rather than the brief, you can delete the brief and regenerate it tomorrow after new intake without losing anything. The brief is disposable; the vault is not. That asymmetry is what makes it a product, not a document.

Two failure shapes to know. A brief that summarizes the chat instead of citing notes looks identical on day one and drifts silently by day five, because it regenerates from conversation memory instead of from the record. And a brief whose citations you never audit becomes a laundering machine: unverified claims acquire the costume of your own system and walk into your operating picture wearing it. The daily habit only compounds if the trails stay walked. At work, this is how a personal knowledge system starts earning its keep daily — the Morning Brief is the first regenerable product, and watchlists, decision logs, and handoff notes follow the same pattern.

Stage 01 · Open the vault, write the bounds

Before the machine touches a note, you establish what it is working toward and what it can reach. Three moves: a LIVE brief, a vault on disk, and a scope the assistant can repeat back to you.

  1. In chat Operator — Direction & Log, paste the brief starter and fill all five fields: Outcome, Done looks like, Bounds, Evidence standard, Stop/hand-back. Name the artifacts — a navigable vault, two cited answers, a Morning Brief note — not "organize my notes." Evidence standard today is specific: every citation opened by you, verdict marked. You should see the AI push back on anything vague; that push-back is the co-writing. If it accepts everything on the first pass, your fields are too vague to fail — tighten Done-looks-like and Evidence standard until it argues. Status LIVE before the mission starts.
  2. Copy mission_flesh/p4/vault_seed/ to a working folder you own (for example Documents\p4-vault) so the course copy stays clean. In Obsidian, choose Open folder as vault and pick that folder. You should see MOC, People, Systems, Events, Decisions, and a 00_Inbox folder holding four captures. If the file list looks wrong, you opened the parent folder — reopen at the folder that directly contains MOC.md.
  3. Start a fresh build chat in the Codex app and open the vault folder as its project, keeping the permission mode at Ask for approval while a machine writes into your knowledge base. Each chat keeps its own project — your pinned operator chats stay in the sidebar on the course project, and you click between chats all morning. Your brief's Bounds field states: read and write only inside the vault, plus the never-leave list (names, incident details). Then prove the scope: ask the assistant to list the files it can see and compare against Explorer. More than the vault means the project is opened too high — reopen at the vault root.

Stage 02 · Triage the inbox into your ontology

Now the raw captures become roads. You direct the organization; the assistant does the typing. The one decision you must not delegate is the ontology — the naming scheme and hub structure the whole vault will follow. People / Systems / Events / Decisions is the seeded default; replace it with your own if your desk thinks differently.

  1. Direct the assistant: propose a title and home for each capture first, write only after you approve. Require [[links]] from the hub notes to every new note, and require status words — rumor, unreproduced, ignore for prod — to survive into the new notes verbatim. You should see the hubs link out to new notes and the graph view (left ribbon in Obsidian) show connections. If you got a dump of AI-named files instead, the ontology was never stated — name it and redirect.

A triage direction that keeps you in charge

Vault root: [path]. Only read and write inside this vault.
Process each file in 00_Inbox into notes under People / Systems / Events / Decisions.
For each capture: propose the note title(s) and links first; wait for my OK.
Preserve status words exactly (rumor, unreproduced, ignore for prod).
Link every new note from its hub and from MOC. Do not delete the originals;
move processed captures to 00_Inbox/processed/.

Stage 03 · Director questions with walkable citations

This is the payoff of the triage: questions no single note can answer. The two questions are in mission_flesh/p4/director_questions.md. Each answer lands as a dated note inside the vault — a [[wiki-link]] is only clickable where Obsidian renders it, and an answer that stays in the chat is just text with brackets. Hold the answers to the standard your brief set — multi-note, cited, status carried — and remember that the machine will happily meet a lower standard if you let it.

  1. Ask: what are the top operational risks this week, and which notes support each? Have the answer written as a note in the vault — Answers/Q1 Risks.md — per the panel below. You should see it appear in Obsidian, claims each citing a [[note]] and spanning several notes: the export, billing, and SSO facts live in different notes, so a real answer must span them. Open the answer note and click two links as a smoke test — real notes, not faded links offering to create one. Single-note answers or unresolved links: failure catalog below, then re-ask.
  2. Ask: what decision is blocked, and what evidence is missing? This one tests whether triage kept the decision open instead of resolving it by paraphrase — the ship-order question has unclear owners, and a good answer says so and names what evidence would unblock it. Same bar, same landing: an answer note in the vault, multiple notes cited, resolvable links. The "what is missing" half is the interesting half; a vault that can say what it does not know is worth more than one that always answers.

Stage 04 · Walk every trail, kill what fails

The audit is where your judgment does the work, and it is yours alone — the assistant does not grade its own citations. Budget real time for it. Fifteen minutes of walking trails teaches you more about machine-generated knowledge than an hour of generating it.

  1. Work from the two answer notes in Obsidian. For every citation in both: click it, read the note, find the sentence that carries the claim, and mark support / partial / not supported — in writing, in your log or an Audit note in the vault. Partial is the verdict to respect: a note recording an unreproduced rumor only partially supports an answer listing it as a live risk. If every verdict comes back support on the first pass, you are probably reading generously — re-check the claims against the exact sentences.
  2. Act on the audit: at least one claim revised or killed because its trail failed — restated as rumor, cut entirely, or re-asked with a tighter direction such as "quote the exact supporting sentence per claim." The reason must be the trail, not vibes, and the before/after goes in your log; the adversarial chat will ask for it. If nothing failed even on a hard second read, tighten the standard until something honest gives — a first audit that catches nothing usually means the bar moved, not the machine.

Stage 05 · Generate the Morning Brief into the vault

Now the machine pattern: a regenerable product living inside the system, not a paste into a document. The brief lands as a note, so it can cite notes — and so tomorrow's version can replace it without losing anything.

  1. Direct the assistant to write Morning_Brief.md into the vault: dated, short, every claim citing a [[note]] — and only claims whose trails you verified in Stage 4. You should see the note appear in Obsidian the moment it is written; that is the shared-files mechanism working. Spot-walk two of its citations before accepting. If the brief quotes the chat instead of citing notes, reject it and regenerate — that difference is exactly what makes it a product instead of a transcript.

A Morning Brief direction that stays a product

Vault root: [path]. Only read and write inside this vault.
Write Morning_Brief.md into the vault: today's date, short.
Every claim cites a [[note]] — vault notes only, never the chat.
Include only claims whose trails I marked support in the audit;
carry status words (rumor, unreproduced). Where the vault is
silent, say so instead of filling the gap.

Questions worth arguing about

  • When is agreement not evidence? Two notes that both trace to the same Slack thread are one source wearing two costumes, and a cited answer built on them looks better-supported than it is. What would your vault have to record — a source line per note? a provenance link? — for the machine to be unable to launder one rumor into three citations?
  • What belongs in the vault versus the chat? Everything-in-the-vault drowns the structure in noise; nothing-in-the-vault starves it. Draw your line: what earned a note today, and what deserved to die in the workroom?
  • Where does "unverified" live? The seed carries a rumor, an unreproduced report, and a note explicitly marked to ignore for production. If your structure has no home for epistemic status, every note ranks as equally true the day retrieval touches it. Is status a word in the text, a tag, a folder — and which of those survives a machine's rewrite?
  • Would you hand this vault to a new deputy on day one? If not, name the gap precisely: missing notes, or missing links? The answer usually embarrasses one specific stage of this morning's work — and tells you which habit to keep.

At your desk

  • Program or project lead: a vault of decisions, owners, and risks; the Monday brief regenerated before standup instead of rebuilt from memory; "what changed since Friday?" becomes a cited answer.
  • Analyst: source notes carrying provenance and status; the standing watch question re-asked each morning; every claim in the product traceable to a source note a reviewer can walk.
  • Consultant: one vault per client, opened as its own project — the boundary is the confidentiality control, and the never-leave list is in the brief before the first question.
  • Ops or on-call lead: incident notes linked to system notes; the handoff brief regenerated at shift change, citing the incidents it summarizes so the next shift can walk what it inherits.

Stretch, once the floor is solid

Three directions, in rough order of value. Reconciliation: ask a question that forces two conflicting notes into the same answer — the director's ask treats the cyber rumor as possibly live while the on-call paste records it as unreproduced — and require the conflict to surface explicitly rather than be smoothed over; smoothing is the failure. Durability: rework the vault's naming and hub structure into an information architecture you would still be using in 90 days, and write down why. Load-bearing honesty: the third question in director_questions.md — where are we over-confident? — or a deliberately loaded question like "confirm the cyber attack is real," answered with trails only, logging a refusal wherever the vault is silent. A system that can decline to answer is the one you can trust when it does answer.

Mastery floor — evidence must survive adversarial review

Mark met / not yet against your own artifacts. If the adversarial pass wounds an item, uncheck it until it is fixed.

  1. Evidence looks like: the brief text names the vault root, the allowed read/write actions, and the never-leave list — written before the mission, not reconstructed after.
  2. Evidence looks like: you can navigate MOC → hub → note by clicking, and the graph view shows a connected structure. A pile of AI-named files with no links is not yet, however tidy the names.
  3. Evidence looks like: both director answers living as notes in the vault, each spanning multiple notes, every citation a clickable link that resolves to a real note. One-note answers are summaries and do not clear the bar.
  4. Evidence looks like: a written mark — support / partial / not supported — for every cited note in both answers, in your log or an Audit note. "I checked them" without marks does not survive the adversarial chat.
  5. Evidence looks like: the before and after of one claim, plus which trail failed and how — restated as rumor, cut, or re-asked. The cause is a verdict from the audit, not a feeling about tone.
  6. Evidence looks like: Morning_Brief.md inside the vault, dated, every claim citing a note — and its citations drawn from trails you already walked, with two spot-checked on acceptance.
  7. Evidence looks like: a log line separating what the machine retrieved from what you judged, and one named thing the vault cannot yet support — a question it would answer thinly, or a status it cannot represent.
  8. Adversarial line present in the log; measurement row written; transfer row written. The test: you could defend this block's verdict to a skeptical peer using only your artifacts, walking any trail they pick.

Pass bar (mastery floor)

MVP is not "it ran." A vault answering with fake or unopened citations is not yet, however smooth the answers read. Evidence must survive adversarial review. Full list: operator/PASS_BARS.md · section P4.

If you get stuck

  • "The assistant can't see my vault." The project is almost always opened at the wrong folder. Reopen it at the vault root — the folder that directly contains MOC.md — then ask the assistant to list what it sees and compare with Explorer.
  • "My answers keep citing one giant note." Triage collapsed the inbox into a single summary file, and a question cannot span notes that do not exist. Split the material back into separate titled notes, link them from the hubs, and re-ask.
  • "My links don't resolve." Wikilinks match note titles exactly — [[Billing API]] and [[Billing-API]] are different notes. Pick one naming convention and rename via Obsidian itself, which updates the links that point at the renamed note.
  • Assistant suddenly erroring mid-morning? A capped API key fails as a 429 quota error that reads like an auth failure. Don't reinstall anything — flag staff and check the key first.
  • When narrowing, narrow the data scope, not the judgment standard: fewer notes, same audit.
