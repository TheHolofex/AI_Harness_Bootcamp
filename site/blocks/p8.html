The craft under the morning

Five pieces of operator craft carry this morning — each one pays off at the stage that leans on it.

Policy before touch

An acceptable-use policy — an AUP — is the written rule set for an endpoint: what may be asked of it, what may not, what data may never leave your laptop on its way there, when a human must approve before results are used, and what gets logged. All week a vendor supplied most of that invisibly. On an operator-governed endpoint, nobody does. The guardrails are yours to author, and an unauthored guardrail is not a guardrail — it's a hope.

The order matters more than the text. A policy written after you've seen the endpoint behave is shaped by what the endpoint did — you end up describing behavior and calling it governance. A policy written before first touch is shaped by your obligations: the data you hold, the people your outputs affect, the failure you can't afford. That's why the AUP carries a version and an effective date, and why the date has to precede your first request. The dated version is also your evidence: anyone auditing the work later can see the rules existed before the behavior they governed.

What going wrong looks like: a policy with an empty Forbidden section, or one so vague it could never refuse anything. Both fail the same way — they cost nothing, so they govern nothing. At work, this discipline is the difference between "we tried the new model" and "we evaluated the new model under a policy I can show you." The second sentence is auditable; the first is not.

The capability tax is a number

Moving off a frontier commercial model usually costs capability. The operator question is never whether there's a tax — it's how much, on which work. You answer that with the frozen suite: the same five case files you scored on Tuesday, unedited, re-run cold on the new endpoint. Each case scores PASS or FAIL against its own checks. A case that passes on both endpoints is a hold. Home PASS turning into open FAIL is a degrade — the tax, made visible. Home FAIL turning into open PASS is an improve, rare and worth reading closely.

The measurement is loop completion, not answer quality. A fluent, confident, beautifully organized answer that misses the case's check is a FAIL; a terse one that hits it is a PASS. That's deliberate: at work you don't ship prose, you ship closed loops, and open models are often better at sounding capable than at closing loops. The frozen part is equally load-bearing — edit a case to be "fairer" to the new model and the comparison stops meaning anything, because you're no longer measuring the same thing twice.

A degrade is a location, not a verdict

When a case degrades, the interesting question is where it broke. Five layers can each kill a case on a new endpoint: the model (the new brain genuinely can't do it), the instructions (your AGENTS.md walls — the standing instructions the harness loads — and your skills never made it onto the re-pointed run), the tests (your check quietly asserts one vendor's formatting habits), the environment (endpoint config, missing tool surface, a path that changed), or the brief (a bound that silently assumed the old model's strengths).

The fix is different at every layer, which is why the label matters. A model-layer failure argues for a partial re-point — keep that workload on the commercial engine. An instructions-layer failure means your craft is portable and you simply haven't ported it yet. A test-layer failure means your suite was never measuring what you thought. The common miss is writing "model" in every row: the new brain gets blamed for a wall that never made the trip. Probe the cheap layers first — environment, then instructions — before you convict the expensive one. At work, this is the skill that turns "the new model is worse" (an argument) into "three of five degrades were harness gaps we can close" (a plan).

A refusal verifies policy ownership

A policy is only load-bearing when it costs you something you wanted. Pick a genuinely useful task that your AUP forbids, ask for it, and record the refusal with the controlling clause. Then run an allowed control task beside it so the record separates governance from endpoint breakage.

Refusing a task nobody would ever run provides no useful evidence. When the policy blocks something it should allow, amend it in writing and bump the version. The rules bind the operator too, and every change leaves a trail.

The method transfers across vendors

What transfers when the vendor changes is not the app — it is the assets: the brief, frozen tests, contract language, logs, and policy. An asset that names an outcome and a check can run against another engine. An asset that depends on one vendor's behavior is a porting job. The matrix and root-cause register show which kind you have.

Two artifacts carry the decision out the door. ENDPOINT_RECOMMENDATION.md turns policy, counts, and unresolved tests into fit/not-fit guidance. The sealed 30-60-90 maps the method onto your desk and records BLOCKED when its evidence is incomplete.

Now paste this at the very top of config.toml in the Notepad window — line 1, above everything already there:

model = "YOUR_MODEL_NAME"
model_provider = "openmodel"

Replace YOUR_MODEL_NAME with the model name from the pin — the quotes stay. If a model = line already exists anywhere in the file, change that line rather than adding a second one.

The placement is TOML law, not tidiness: every line below a [bracketed] header belongs to that header's table, so these two lines sit above the first header to stay root keys — and for the same reason, the provider table you add next goes below everything, never above.

Now paste this at the very bottom of config.toml, below everything already in the file, then save:

[model_providers.openmodel]
name = "Hosted open model"
base_url = "YOUR_ENDPOINT"
env_key = "OPEN_MODEL_API_KEY"
wire_api = "responses"

Replace YOUR_ENDPOINT with the endpoint from the pin — the quotes stay. Before you save, scroll the file once: every line that was there before — forced_login_method = "api" included — must still sit above this new header. A pre-existing line that ended up below it has silently joined the table and stopped doing its job; move the table back to the bottom and save again.

Line by line: model names what to ask for, model_provider routes requests through the openmodel entry, base_url is where they go, and env_key names the environment variable that will hold the key — the key itself never goes in this file. wire_api = "responses" declares the endpoint speaks the Responses API, the only protocol the app supports.

Operational questions

  • What degrade count would you accept? If open holds 3/5, define which low-stakes work remains fit and which shipped work stays out.
  • Who writes the AUP at work — you, security, or legal? Probably all three eventually. But an operator-authored draft, dated before first touch, changes the conversation: you arrive with governance to review instead of behavior to excuse.
  • Is an improve cell good news? A case your home model failed that the open model passes says something — but about which layer? Before celebrating, ask whether the test was measuring capability or one vendor's habits.
  • What would you not re-point even if every number held? Some workloads stay put for reasons no matrix captures — data classification, contractual terms, a person downstream. A portfolio answer beats a loyalty answer.

What this looks like on a real desk

  • Engineering desk: the vendor deprecates your model mid-quarter. You already have a frozen suite; migration day is a cold run and a matrix, and the memo to your lead is counts plus layer labels — not "the new one seems fine."
  • Mission-ops desk: a policy shop bans external endpoints for one data class. Your AUP plus refuse record is the compliance story that already exists; the partial re-point strategy — what stays commercial and why — is the plan you table the same day.
  • Management desk: model-choice briefings use the recommendation's shape — decision, workload split, totals, policy version, conditions, owner, and residual risk.
