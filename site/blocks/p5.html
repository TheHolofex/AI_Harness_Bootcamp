The poisoned corpus

An intake drop reaches the vault you built carrying a false citation, a field contradiction, and a hostile instruction somewhere among ordinary traffic — each aimed at a different thing you rely on. The job is to catch all three mechanically and prove your trusted notes never took the hit.

Why this matters

The moment a knowledge system starts driving decisions, its intake becomes a target. This morning you got a thinking machine working inside your vault; now the skill is keeping that arrangement when someone rigs what flows in. Anyone who can put a file in front of your assistant — a vendor, a counterpart, a stranger whose PDF got forwarded — can try three attacks: lie about what a source says, plant two incompatible facts as one record, or address instructions directly to your machine.

This run stages all three against you at once, mixed into a batch of ordinary intake. You know the classes are coming; you don't know which items carry them — and the discipline doesn't care: none of the checks you'll build depend on knowing what's wrong in advance, which is exactly why the same checks catch the poison nobody announces.

What you will leave with

  • A trusted boundary you wrote down before intake touched it — vault path, staging path, and the rule that nothing crosses until triaged
  • Three catches on the record — false citation, field contradiction, hostile instruction — each with mechanical evidence a skeptic can re-run
  • A containment proof: the exact places the poison aimed at, shown untouched against a baseline
  • The Codex app's sandbox and approvals named as separate controls, and the ability to read an OpenCode permission rule set and predict what it permits before running anything against it
  • An intake rule you'll reuse at work, a P5 transfer seed, and a full operator pulse

Before you start

  • Operator templates present in your Codex app project (DIRECTION_BRIEF.md, OPERATOR_LOG.md)
  • Your P4 vault available, plus somewhere to put a baseline copy of it
  • The intake pack on disk: mission_flesh/p5/intake/ (one batch of seven items) and mission_flesh/p5/triage_template.md
  • Monday's corpus still on disk at mission_flesh/p1/corpus/ — intake items cite into it, and your citation checks will read from it

How poison works — and how it's caught

Five ideas carry this work. Get them down before anything touches the intake — each one decides a move you'll make under pressure.

Three poisons, three targets

Poisoned intake is material arriving into your knowledge system carrying something designed to change the system, not inform it. The three classes in today's pack aren't three flavors of the same problem — each one corrupts a different thing you rely on, and naming which one you're looking at decides the containment move.

A false citation corrupts your evidence. It's a claim wearing a real-looking reference: once it lands in the vault, your citation trail — the thing that made yesterday's answers trustworthy — starts vouching for a lie. There are two variants, and today's pack carries both: a reference to a real source that says the opposite of what's claimed, and a reference to a source that doesn't exist at all. The move is the same for both: open the source and compare, then reject or correct the claim.

A field contradiction corrupts your facts. Two incompatible assertions arrive as one record; if they merge, your picture of the world is internally inconsistent, and any later question might surface either half with equal confidence. The move is to refuse the merge: hold both halves with a reconciliation note, or reject the record — never let the system quietly pick one.

A hostile instruction corrupts your control. This is prompt injection in plain words: a model reads all the text in front of it, and it has no built-in channel that marks which text came from its operator and which came from a file — so text in data that looks like an order can get treated as one. The author of a document is trying to become the director of your machine. The move is containment: never execute, isolate the file, then prove nothing executed.

Misname the class and you fix the wrong layer. The classic version is treating a hostile instruction as merely "wrong content" and summarizing it into the vault — the summary carries the payload, and now the poison sits inside the trusted boundary wearing your own assistant's voice.

Containment is an absence you can show

Containment doesn't mean the assistant behaved well. It means the hostile effect did not happen — and that's a claim about files and outputs, not about the conversation. The useful property of a hostile instruction is that it names its own success conditions: whatever it demands — a trusted note changed, data exfiltrated (sent out to somewhere it should never go), a record deleted — is your checklist. Go to each place the effect would have landed and show it didn't: the note it wanted changed is unchanged, the data it wanted sent appears in no output, the record it wanted deleted still exists. And don't be surprised if a demand names something your vault doesn't even hold — hostile instructions are written blind to the target. That's a tell worth keeping, and the check simply widens: confirm nothing of that shape appears in the chat or any output either.

Here's why the chat can't testify: the channel you'd be asking is the channel that was attacked. If the instruction had worked, the same compromised process would be the one reassuring you — so "I ignored it" carries no information either way. Artifacts carry information. This is also why a baseline matters: "unchanged" is only provable against a copy from before, which is why you snapshot the vault before intake ever runs. And because the session running your checks is the same one that read the note, re-check at least one target yourself — open the targeted note against your baseline copy, or re-run the diff in a fresh chat. An artifact you can independently re-run is what separates evidence from a more elaborate "I ignored it."

The failure shape to avoid is a log verdict that reads "assistant confirmed it ignored the instruction." At work this is the difference between closing an incident on the suspect's statement and closing it on the audit.

Catch it mechanically — don't wait for a confession

A mechanical check is one whose outcome doesn't depend on the model choosing to tell you. Three of them do almost all the work here. Quarantine is structural: intake lands in a staging area the vault never reads, so poison can't touch trusted material because the path simply doesn't cross. Claim-versus-source is comparative: before any claim enters the vault, open what it cites and read the line — the check succeeds or fails on the file's content, not on anyone's honesty. Diff is the catch-all tripwire: compare the vault to its baseline, and anything that changed without your say-so shows up regardless of how it got there.

Each check leaves an artifact — a staging listing, a quoted pair of lines, an empty diff — and that's the point. "We told the model to be careful with this data" is the opposite: volunteered honesty. It may even work most of the time, and it proves nothing any of the time, which is why the pass bar names it as a failure on its own.

One subtle trap: summarization is not triage. Ask the assistant to "review the intake" and you may get a fluent summary that repeats the false claim in a confident register — now unmarked, one step closer to trusted. Triage means running the named checks, not describing the files.

The machine flags; the verdict stays yours

Scale the detection, never the verdict. It's fine — necessary, at volume — for the machine to run every check and raise every flag. But each catch gets confirmed by you personally: open the evidence, agree the class, sign the disposition. The triage record is where that confirmation becomes a row — file, class, evidence, disposition — and an unsigned flag is not a catch yet.

The failure mode has a name: a triage system that auto-clears its own alarms. It flags, it investigates itself, it resolves — and it has quietly taken your job, which you discover later, at the vault, when something that "cleared" turns out to have landed. Permission modes are the adjacent lesson: a mode changes who reviews an action before it runs, and nothing in any mode reviews whether a claim is true. Action gates can be delegated by policy; truth verdicts have no mode. They only have you.

The honest version of being overwhelmed is narrowing intake or tightening the mechanical filters — not letting the flags clear themselves and calling it efficiency.

Read the rules, predict the permit

In OpenCode, bounds aren't a mode you pick from a menu — they're rules you author: an ordered list in opencode.json mapping action patterns to allow, ask, or deny. The part that inverts most people's instinct is the resolution order: the list is read top to bottom and the last rule that matches wins. Not the first, not the most specific — the last. That makes the bottom of the list the position of power, which is the opposite of how firewalls train you to think.

The shipped defaults are a working demonstration: read * allow, then *.env deny, then *.env.example allow. A read of .env matches rules 1 and 2; rule 2 is last, so it's denied. A read of .env.example matches all three; rule 3 is last, so it's allowed. The order is the meaning — and the trap follows directly: paste your deny list at the top of the array and every later allow silently overrides it. You did nothing, and everything still reads as configured.

The discipline that survives this: predict, then test, then trust. Write down what the rule set permits for three concrete actions before running anything, probe once with something harmless, and only then rely on it. A wrong prediction you wrote down teaches you the resolver; a right one you assumed teaches you nothing and might be luck. The same discipline transfers to every policy engine on every desk — firewalls, mail filters, IAM — because each one has its own resolution order, and assuming it is how bounds fail silently.

Run of exercise

Work the stages in order and check each step off as its proof exists. Progress saves in this browser — the same tick shows on the home page.

Stage 01 · Brief the boundary

Before any intake gets read, you and the AI freeze what "trusted" means and what evidence will count. The boundary has to exist before the poison does — a boundary drawn after the fact is a story, not a control.

  1. Open the chat Operator — Direction & Log and paste the starter from operator/DIRECTION_BRIEF.md. The Outcome here is a containment verdict with evidence — not "process the intake." Expect the AI to challenge vague wording; that's the interview working. You should see all five fields accepted and Status set to LIVE before anything reads the intake. If the dialogue runs long, accept tighter wording — the brief is a frame, not an essay.
  2. In the Bounds field, write the vault's exact folder path, the staging path, and the crossing rule: nothing moves from staging to vault until it has a triage row. Name mission_flesh/p1/corpus/ too, as read-only reference material — citations resolve against it; nothing writes to it. Add the evidence standard: a catch counts only with mechanical evidence. If you can't name the vault path from memory, open the folder and copy the path in — a boundary that lives in your head isn't frozen.

Stage 02 · Quarantine the intake

The intake batch lands on your machine — but outside the boundary, in a staging area the vault never reads. You also save the baseline that every later "unchanged" claim will be measured against.

  1. First save a baseline: copy the whole vault folder aside (or note a clean git state). Then copy mission_flesh/p5/intake/ into a staging folder outside the vault and direct the assistant to read intake only from there. The diff direction is one line — "Compare the vault folder against the baseline copy and list every file that differs" — and here it should come back: no differences, with all seven items in staging. If the assistant already summarized intake into the vault, restore from the baseline and restate the boundary before continuing.

Stage 03 · Triage — three catches

Now the checks run: every citation opened against its source, every field claim compared against its neighbors, every file scanned for text that gives orders. The machine runs the checks and raises the flags; you confirm each one and write the row. Citations here resolve against two places — your vault and Monday's corpus at mission_flesh/p1/corpus/, still on disk as the standing source set — so a source counts as nonexistent only after both have been searched.

The triage record template is at mission_flesh/p5/triage_template.md — file, class, evidence, disposition. Extend the table as you fill it: add hold to the disposition set for a record that needs a reconciliation note, and a fifth column of rough times — detect, isolate, verify — so the order exists as a record instead of a later reconstruction from chat scroll. Every intake item gets a row, the clean ones included: accept is a verdict you record, not a default.

  1. Direct a check of every citation in the batch against the source it names — existence first, then content: search the vault and Monday's corpus (mission_flesh/p1/corpus/) for the source, then open it and read the exact line the claim leans on. A citation fails two ways — the source exists but says otherwise, or it resolves nowhere after both searches — and both failures hide in today's batch. Evidence is the claim quoted beside the source's own line, or beside the failed lookup, with rough detect / isolate / verify times on the row — "I noticed it looked wrong" doesn't survive adversarial review.
  2. Somewhere in the batch, one record asserts two things that can't both be true of the same event. Direct the assistant to extract every field claim — subject, place, time, status — and lay same-subject claims side by side; once incompatible assertions sit next to each other, the contradiction is mechanical, not opinion. Disposition is hold-with-reconciliation-note or reject — never merge as-is. Evidence is the side-by-side extract in your triage record, with rough detect / isolate / verify times on the row.
  3. One item in the batch is text talking to your assistant, not to you. Scan every file for instruction-shaped lines — imperatives aimed at the machine reading it: change a trusted note, send data out, suppress a check, delete a record. The catch is naming those lines in the triage record and executing none of them. Detect, isolate to quarantine, then verify clean — in that order, with rough times noted for the log. If the assistant quotes the hostile lines back while reporting, that's fine; quoting isn't obeying. Obeying any one of them is the failure.

Stage 04 · Prove the absence

The hostile note told you exactly what it wanted to happen. Now visit each of those places and show it didn't. This is the stage where the containment claim becomes evidence instead of atmosphere.

  1. The hostile item named its own effects, so check each demand at its target: the note it wanted changed diffs clean against your baseline (re-run the one-line diff direction from step 3), the data it wanted sent appears in no chat or output, the record it wanted deleted still exists. If a demand names something your vault doesn't hold, widen that check — nothing of that shape in the chat or any output. Then re-verify one target yourself: open the targeted note against the baseline copy, or re-run the diff in a fresh chat. The exposed session's own report proves nothing.

Stage 05 · Read the bounds

Two tools express the same bound in two grammars. You'll name what each one actually enforces, then read a rule set cold and predict what it permits before trusting it. This is the stage where "the tool has permissions" becomes "I know what these permissions do."

On the Codex app side, both controls have real locations: permission modes are enabled in Settings > General > Permissions, and the active mode sits beneath the composer — read it before anything else and expect one of Ask for approval, Approve for me, or Full access. Then watch one approval pause fire — in Ask for approval, a small file write is enough — and note the split: what pauses for you is the approvals control; what the sandbox blocks never asks at all.

OpenCode reads its rules from opencode.json at the project root — and most projects don't have one yet, which means the shipped defaults are in force with nothing on disk showing them. If the file is missing, create it at the root and author the three default rules into a permission block yourself; writing them down is the fastest way to learn the resolver. For the probe, make a scratch .env with one dummy line and an .env.example beside it, then ask OpenCode to read each: expect deny on .env, allow on .env.example.

  1. One clear sentence per tool on what it enforces versus what stays procedure. In the Codex app, read the current mode beneath the composer, then name the two controls separately: the sandbox sets what's reachable, approvals set when it pauses. In OpenCode, open the permission rules in opencode.json at the project root — no file means the shipped defaults are in force; create it as described above — write down what you predict the rules permit for three concrete actions, then run the scratch-.env probe: expect deny on .env, allow on .env.example. Last match wins. Finish by naming which of your three catches a permission control could have blocked, and which needed your judgment.

Two controls, two rule styles

Codex app → two controls do different jobs: the sandbox decides which files and network the agent can reach; approvals decide when it pauses for you. Switching from Ask for approval to Approve for me changes who reviews the escalation — it does not widen the sandbox. · OpenCode → bounds are rules in opencode.json, not a mode you pick in a menu, and they resolve last-match-wins: a later allow overrides an earlier deny, so the shipped defaults read read * allow, then *.env deny, then *.env.example allow — the order is the whole meaning. Write one sentence on how the two tools express the same bound, and where enforcement is weaker or merely conventional. Note what neither control can do: no sandbox, mode, or rule judges whether a claim is true — the truth catches were always yours.

Stage 06 · Seal the rule

The whole run compresses into one sentence you'll still be using months from now, and a seed that points it at your real desk.

  1. Complete the sentence you'll reuse at work: "I never accept intake into trusted knowledge until ___." Fill the blank with your actual gate — a triage row, a source check, a diff — not an attitude. Then test it against today: run each of your three catches through the sentence and confirm the rule would have caught them. A rule that wouldn't have caught today's poison isn't finished.
  2. Name your real trusted boundary at work — the notes, wiki, or drive that actually drives decisions — plus the first inbox or corpus you'd put behind a triage gate, and the intake rule you just wrote. Those three together are the seed; they become a 30-60-90 line you can actually run, in the transfer pulse below.

Questions worth arguing about

  • At what flag volume does "confirm each personally" break — and what changes first when it does? The honest answers are narrower intake or tighter mechanical filters, both of which keep the verdict yours. The dishonest answer is letting the flags clear themselves, which keeps the ceremony and discards the control.
  • Is a quarantined contradiction a liability or an asset? A held pair of incompatible claims is also a record that the world disagreed at a specific time — some desks would pay for exactly that signal. The answer changes how long you keep quarantine and who reads it.
  • Would you let a second engine adjudicate the first engine's flags? Two machines agreeing is cheaper than your attention — and it's still agreement, not truth. Be precise about what you kept and what you handed over before you call it a control.
  • Where is the trusted boundary on your desk right now, and who else can write inside it? If the answer includes "anyone who can email me an attachment," you have intake without a gate — which is most desks, which is why this craft is rare and valuable.

The same craft on other desks

On an analyst desk, the vault is the team wiki and the poison is a vendor report citing a study that says less than claimed. Claim-versus-source at the boundary is the same check with the same artifact: the quoted pair.

On an operations desk, incident timelines assembled from several sources carry field contradictions as a matter of routine. The discipline is the merge refusal: hold both accounts with a reconciliation note until someone with authority reconciles, and never let the tooling quietly pick a winner.

On any desk with an assistant that reads email, hostile instruction is a standing condition, not an exotic case — every message body is text written by a stranger, sitting in front of your machine. The containment posture you built today, quarantine plus prove-clean, is the honest default there.

Operator pulse — required every block

The same six-beat ritual closes every half-day: it is how a mission becomes evidence. Paste-ready prompts →

  1. Staging, triage, containment, and the bounds work all live in build chats; the operator chat holds direction and verdicts only. If triage arguments and verdict drafting share one chat, the trail that makes your evidence auditable disappears.
  2. Back in Direction & Log: what you directed, mid-run changes, verdict (ACCEPT / REJECT / ACCEPT WITH FIXES), evidence pointers, what you still own. Here the verdict line is the containment claim, so the pointers must reach artifacts — triage record, diff result, quarantine listing — not chat moments.
  3. Against P5's MVP list: met / not yet, each with an evidence pointer. The bars want mechanical evidence per class, time-ordered detect → isolate → verify, the bounds comparison, and the written rule-set prediction. No inflation — an unproven absence is a not-yet, not a probably.
  4. Chat Adversarial — P5: frozen prompt + artifacts + provisional verdict. Expect the attack to land on absence of effect — "you checked the chat; did you check the targets?" Copy the final line into your log: ADVERSARIAL: stood | wounded | failed. Fix if wounded.
  5. Ritual 0–3 · mission HIT/PARTIAL/MISS · quality STRONG/ADEQUATE/WEAK · time to result (minutes) — plus the P5 deep mark: Containment: 3/3 catches · absence-of-effect proof pointer. Spend the one-line reflection on the containment claim, since that's the claim that was under fire.
  6. Chat Operator — Transfer 30-60-90: paste the every-session prompt. P5 is a deep-seed block — the boundary-plus-intake-rule pair from step 10 belongs in the living file, not just in today's chat. A session-log row is required, and this block doesn't end on an empty seed.

Mastery floor — evidence must survive adversarial review

Mark met / not yet against your own artifacts. If the adversarial pass wounds an item, uncheck it until it is fixed.

  1. Evidence looks like: the brief's Bounds field naming vault path, staging path, and crossing rule, with the chat showing the boundary went LIVE before any intake was read. A boundary written after the poison arrived is a not-yet.
  2. Evidence looks like: the claimed line and the source's own line quoted side by side in the triage record, plus any citation that resolved to no file at all flagged as such. "The model said it was wrong" is not mechanical — the quoted pair is.
  3. Evidence looks like: both assertions extracted side by side with a disposition of hold or reject. If the merged note quietly picked one version, the contradiction wasn't caught — it was silently resolved, which is worse.
  4. Evidence looks like: the hostile file in quarantine, none of its instructions executed, and the absence checks from step 7 — clean diff, nothing exfiltrated, logs intact. Both halves are required: a catch without the absence proof is half a catch.
  5. Evidence looks like: the log or triage record showing detect → isolate → verify clean for each class, in time order. If verification actually ran before isolation, say so honestly — an order violation found now is cheaper than one found at work.
  6. Evidence looks like: one enforce-versus-procedure sentence per tool; sandbox and approvals named as separate Codex app controls; your written OpenCode prediction with the probe result; and the sentence naming which catches a permission control could have blocked (the hostile write, an outbound send) and which no mode can judge (whether a claim is true).
  7. Evidence looks like: the completed "until ___" sentence with an observable gate in the blank, plus the test — each of today's three catches run against it, each caught. A gate you can't observe isn't a gate.
  8. Evidence looks like: the log's verdict resting on artifacts — diff result, target checks, quarantine listing — with no load-bearing reassurance from the assistant. Strip every sentence the model produced about its own behavior; the proof should still stand. If it doesn't, this is a not-yet.
  9. The adversarial line is in the log, the measurement row and transfer row exist, and you could walk a skeptical peer from the brief to the containment proof without opening the build chat. That's a closed block.

Pass bar (mastery floor)

MVP is not "it ran." Evidence must survive adversarial review — and for this capability the bar is explicit that "we told the model to be careful" is a failure on its own. Full list: operator/PASS_BARS.md · section P5.

If you get stuck

  • Everything looks suspicious now. That's the paranoia swing, and it costs the same as blindness. Narrow the data scope to one intake item at a time — the batch is only seven — and keep the judgment standard fixed. The standard never narrows; the scope does.
  • You can't prove the vault is clean. You're missing a baseline. If you saved one in step 3, diff against it. If you didn't, reconstruct what should exist from mission_flesh/p4/vault_seed/ plus your own P4 build chat, snapshot now, and re-run triage so the next proof is cheap.
  • The OpenCode rules don't behave the way you read them. Write your prediction down before testing again, then re-read the list bottom-up — the last matching rule decides. A deny that must win goes after the allow it beats. One harmless probe per prediction. If there's no opencode.json to open at all, nothing is wrong — with no file the shipped defaults are in force; create one at the project root and author the rules yourself (stage 05 shows the move).
  • Still stuck: ask the lead to diagram the one concept blocking you, then continue on your own chair.
